Velocity Profile Development — No-Slip, Entrance & Fully Developed

The wall enforces the no-slip condition (u = 0 at r = R). At the pipe inlet the velocity is nearly uniform (plug flow). Viscous shear gradually slows the fluid near the wall and accelerates the core, so the profile sharpens until it becomes fully developed at x = Le. For laminar flow, Vmax = 2 Vavg; for turbulent, Vmax ≈ 1.22 Vavg.

Note: the x/D stations are drawn on a schematic (index) scale, evenly spaced for clarity — not to linear scale.

Pressure Distribution — Entrance vs Fully Developed

Bernoulli: where velocity is low, pressure is high — the entrance region (uniform, low‑velocity profile) has the highest pressure.

Why P drops: viscous shear converts mechanical energy into heat. The entrance region has extra loss because the velocity profile is still rearranging, so dP/dx is steeper there than in the fully developed section.

Pressure Along the Whole Apparatus — Why P is High at Inlet, Why it Drops

The curve shows the piezometric head at every point along the apparatus. The shaded bands mark the three measuring devices. Pressure always drops in the flow direction — but locally it recovers in the venturi's diverging cone, and never fully recovers after the orifice or the elbow.
● Tapping point ── Piezometric head H(x) ▨ Device zone ╌╌ Venturi Δh ╌╌ Orifice Δh ╌╌ Elbow Δh

Venturi — Pressure Recovers

Converging: velocity rises, static pressure falls (Bernoulli).
Throat: highest velocity, lowest pressure.
Diverging: velocity falls, pressure recovers — about 80–90% because the smooth cone suppresses separation. Overall permanent loss is small (Cd = 0.98).

Orifice — Irreversible Loss

Plate: the jet contracts sharply and separates at the edges.
Vena contracta: minimum pressure, minimum jet area (≈62% of hole).
Recovery: only partial — large recirculation zones dissipate energy. Permanent loss is high (Cd = 0.63).

90° Elbow — Loss Coefficient

Why P drops at a bend: the fluid must change direction, so the outer wall experiences a favourable pressure gradient and the inner wall an adverse one. The adverse gradient causes separation, which feeds secondary (Dean) vortices and dissipates energy. The drop scales with K · V²/2g with K ≈ 0.84. There is no pressure recovery downstream — the loss is permanent.

How Flow Rate Affects Pressure Drop

For incompressible flow through any of these devices, the head loss scales as Δh ∝ Q². Doubling the flow rate quadruples the pressure drop. That is why the curves below are parabolas, and why flowmeters are calibrated as Q = k √(Δh).

Why the Pressure Behaves This Way — Summary

1. Why is P high at the inlet?
Bernoulli's equation says P + ½ρV² + ρgz = constant. At the inlet the fluid is still nearly uniform (plug-like), so V is low, therefore P is high. As the flow develops, wall shear and the boundary layer reduce the effective cross-section, so V in the core rises and P falls.

2. Why does P drop through the venturi?
The converging cone forces V to rise (continuity), so by Bernoulli P must fall. The minimum P is at the throat. In the diverging cone V falls again and P recovers — almost all of it, because the smooth geometry suppresses separation. Only a small permanent loss remains.

3. Why does P drop much more at the orifice?
A sharp-edged plate forces the jet to separate. The minimum area is not at the hole but downstream, at the vena contracta (≈62% of the hole). So the peak velocity — and therefore the minimum pressure — is even lower than the hole geometry suggests. Downstream, large recirculation zones dissipate energy, so pressure does not recover.

4. Why does the elbow cause a permanent loss?
Turning the flow creates an adverse pressure gradient on the inner wall. The boundary layer there separates, generating secondary (Dean) vortices that convert kinetic energy into heat. The drop is Δh = K·V²/2g with K ≈ 0.84.

5. How does flow rate affect the drops?
Continuity gives V ∝ Q, so all head losses scale as V², i.e. Δh ∝ Q². At low flow rate the drops are small and the flow may be laminar; at high flow rate the drops grow quadratically and the flow becomes turbulent.

6. Why is the elbow's loss much smaller than the orifice's?
A 90° elbow changes direction but keeps the same cross-section, so the velocity is never forced above its pipe value. An orifice must pass the whole flow through a much smaller area, so Vpeak/Vpipe ≈ 2.5, and Δh scales as V² — roughly 6× larger.

7. Where is the flow laminar, transitional, or turbulent?
It depends on both V and D. At low flow rates the pipe is laminar (Re < 2300) but the throat and vena contracta may already be transitional or turbulent. At high flow rates every section is turbulent. See the live table above.
